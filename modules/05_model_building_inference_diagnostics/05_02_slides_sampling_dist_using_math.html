Analytical Solution – Fixed x

  • Consider a simple linear regression \(y_{i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i} + e_{i}.\)

  • Assume the errors \(e_{i}\) are independently and identically distributed (iid) with mean \(0\) and variance \(\sigma^{2}\).

  • Statistical theory then provides an exact formula for the standard errors:

\[\text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i} - \bar{x})^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n \text{var}(x)}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n} s_{x}}\]

  • Key determinants: Standard error decreases (giving us more precise estimates) when:

    • Smaller \(\sigma\) (error variability): Less dispersion of the data points around the true regression line.

    • Larger \(n\) (sample size): More observations naturally narrow our uncertainty.

    • Larger \(s_{x}\) (spread of \(x\)): More horizontal variation in our predictor acts as a stronger lever to pin down the slope.

Analytical Solution – Random x

  • Consider a simple linear regression \(y_{i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i} + e_{i}.\)

  • Assume the errors \(e_{i}\) are independently and identically distributed (iid) with mean \(0\) and variance \(\sigma^{2}\).

  • Statistical theory then provides an exact formula for the standard errors:

\[\text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = E\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i} - \bar{x})^2}}\right) = E\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n \text{var}(x)}}\right) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} E\left(\frac{1}{s_{x}}\right)\]

  • Key determinants: Standard error decreases (giving us more precise estimates) when:

    • Smaller \(\sigma\) (error variability): Less dispersion of the data points around the true regression line.

    • Larger \(n\) (sample size): More observations naturally narrow our uncertainty.

    • Larger \(E(1/s_{x})\) (spread of \(x\)): More horizontal variation in our predictor acts as a stronger lever to pin down the slope.

Analytical Solution – Practical Challenges

\[\text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i} - \bar{x})^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n \text{var}(x)}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n} s_{x}}\]

  • Problem: \(\sigma\) is also unknown – just like \(\beta_{0}\) and \(\beta_{1}\).

  • Solution: estimate error variance \(\sigma^{2} = \text{var}(e)\) in the population using the residual variance \(\widehat{\sigma}^{2} = \text{var}(\widehat{e})\) in the sample.

    • \(\widehat{\sigma}^{2} = \text{var}(\widehat{e}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \widehat{e}_{i}^{2} = \text{MSE} = \text{RMSE}^{2} = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n},\) where \(\text{RSS} = \text{Resiual Sum of Squares} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \widehat{e}_{i}^{2}\).

    • In practice, we use: \(\widehat{\sigma}^{2} = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n-2}.\)

    • (More generally, we use \(\widehat{\sigma}^{2} = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n-p-1}\) when we have \(p\) predictors \(x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\).)

Step 1 — Compute the Residual Sum of Squares

\[\text{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^n \widehat{e}_i^{\,2}\]

Step 2 — Divide by the residual degrees of freedom

\[\widehat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - 2}\]

(With \(p\) predictors: \(\widehat{\sigma}^2 = \text{RSS}/(n - p - 1)\))

Step 3 — Plug in

\[\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}\, s_{x}}\]

  • \(\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_1)\) can now be calculated based on the (only) sample we have.

Analytical Solution – Remaining Issues

\[\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n} s_{x}}\]

  • While \(\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_1)\) can now be calculated based on the (only) sample we have,
    • we can only use the observed \(x\) values – calculations for random \(x\) will require additional theoretical assumptions and derivations/simulations;
    • it will now also have its own sampling fluctuations since \(\widehat{\sigma}\) (as well \(s_{x}\), when \(x\) is random) will vary across repeated samples – just like \(\widehat{\beta}_1\) almost never exactly equals \(\beta_1\), \(\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_1)\) will not exactly equal \({\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_1)\).

From Standard Errors to Confidence Intervals

  • For any given sample, the estimated slope \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) will almost certainly not equal the true slope \(\beta_{1}\).

  • Reporting just the point estimate \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) is thus not enough.

  • We can report the standard error \(\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})\), which measures the variability of \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) across repeated samples.

  • We can also report a confidence interval for \(\beta_{1}\), which provides a plausible range of values for \(\beta_{1}\).

  • We previously made the following two observations about the sampling distribution of \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\):

    • Shape: The sampling distribution of \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) is unimodal and bell-shaped.

    • Coverage: Approximately \(95\%\) of the time \(\widehat{\beta}_{1} \in [\beta_{1} \pm 2 \text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})]\).

  • We can exploit these observations to construct an approximate \(95\%\) confidence interval for \(\beta_{1}\) as:

\[ 95\% \text{ CI for } \beta_{1}: [\widehat{\beta}_{1} \pm 2 \times \widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})] \]

Confidence Intervals – Interpretation

\[ 95\% \text{ CI for } \beta_{1}: [\widehat{\beta}_{1} \pm 2 \times \widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})] \]

  • What does “95% Confidence” actually mean here?

    • If we collect \(100\) independent samples and construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for each, approximately \(95\) of those intervals will capture the true population parameter \(\beta_{1}\).

    • It is the interval that is random, not the population parameter!

  • What are the key trade-offs?

    • To be more confident (>\(95\%\)): We must increase the length of the interval with a larger multiplier (\(>2\)).
    • To get a narrower interval (more precision): We need a smaller standard error (which we get from a larger \(n\), lower error variability \(\sigma\), or wider spread of x-values \(s_x\)).

Confidence Intervals – Analytical Solution

  • First, we can use the analytical approach to estimating \({\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})\).

  • Second, we can use additional theoretical assumptions and derivations to refine the formula for confidence intervals.

  • Specifically, if we assume

    • \(e_{i}\) are iid with mean \(0\) and (unknown) variance \(\sigma^{2}\) (original assumption),
    • \(e_{i}\) are normally distributed (new assumption),

    the formula for \(95\%\) confidence interval for \(\beta_1\) can refined as

\[ 95\% \text{ CI for } \beta_{1}: [\widehat{\beta}_{1} \pm t_{n-2,0.025}^{*} \times \widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})] \]

  • Here \(t^{*}_{df,\alpha}\) is the upper \(\alpha\%\) critical value from a \(t\)-distribution with degrees of freedom \(df\).
    • For simple linear regression with one predictor \(x\), \(df = n-2\).
    • (For multiple linear regression with \(p\) predictors \(x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\), \(df = n-p-1\).)
    • For large \(n\), \(t\)-distribution closely resembles a normal distribution and \(t^* \approx 1.96\).

Uncertainty Quantification – Analytical Solution – Pros and Cons

Strengths

  • Exact under the stated assumptions
  • Fast — no simulation needed
  • Foundation of classical \(t\)-tests and \(F\)-tests
  • Widely implemented in all statistical software

Limitations

  • Requires homoscedasticity (constant \(\sigma^2\))
  • Requires normality of errors
  • Breaks down with correlated errors
  • Can be unreliable in small samples
