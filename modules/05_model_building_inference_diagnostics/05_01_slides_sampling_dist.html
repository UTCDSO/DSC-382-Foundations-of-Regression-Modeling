Statistical Uncertainty in Regression

Sampling Distribution and Standard Error

James Scott | Abhra Sarkar | UT-Austin

Sampling Variability

  • We want to study the relationship between hours studied and exam scores.
  • We collect a random sample of 100 UT-Austin students.
  • We fit a regression: \(y_{i} = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i} + e_{i},\) where \(y \equiv \text{exam scores}\) and \(x \equiv \text{hours studied}.\)
  • Suppose we get: \(\widehat{\beta}_{1} = 2.46.\)
  • Each additional hour of studying raises the predicted score by 2.46 points.

What if we had sampled a different set of 100 students — would we still get the same estimate \(\widehat{\beta}_{1} = 2.46\)?

No. Our sample is just one of many possible samples from the population. A different sample would give a different \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\).

How confident can we then be in that number \(\widehat{\beta}_{1} = 2.46\)? To find out, we need to quantify its sampling variability.

Sampling Distribution and Standard Error

  • We can quantify our ‘confidence’ in our estimate \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) by studying its sampling distribution.

  • More specifically, we are interested in the variability or spread of the sampling distribution.

  • Naturally, we can measure this by the standard deviation of \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) in its sampling distribution.

  • This is also called the standard error of \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\), denoted \(\text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})\).

\[\text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_{1}) \equiv \text{standard deviation of } \widehat{\beta}_{1} \text{ in repeated samples}\]

  • Why call it an ‘error’? Because it measures the average ‘error’ in estimating the true \({\beta}_{1}\) across repeated samples.

Sampling Distribution – Shape and Coverage

  • Shape: The sampling distribution of \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) is unimodal and bell-shaped.

  • Coverage: Approximately \(95\%\) of the \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) values fall within \([\beta_{1} \pm 2 \text{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_{1})]\) limits.

Practical Challenges

  • Repeated sampling from the population is essentially a thought experiment.

  • In real life:

    • We have to almost always work with only one sample.
    • We cannot go back and resample from the population.
    • So we cannot directly observe the sampling distribution.

  1. The classical analytical solution: Clever mathematical tricks that derive an analytical formula for the SE under strict theoretical assumptions (such as normality or a known error structure), and then estimate the SE by applying those formula to the (only) sample we have.

  2. The modern algorithmic solution: Clever computational tricks (like bootstrapping) that approximate the SE by repeatedly sampling from the (only) sample we have, and then estimate the SE empirically from all those resamples.
